Electricity in Nigeria compared with similar countries

  • Electricity
  • Our World in Data
  • 220 countries
  • 2000 to 2024
03_then_and_now.sql
-- 03_then_and_now.sql
-- Where each peer started (2000), where it is now (2024), and how fast it grew.
WITH ends AS (
    SELECT
        country,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2000 THEN kwh_per_person END) AS kwh_2000,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2024 THEN kwh_per_person END) AS kwh_2024,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2000 THEN population END) AS pop_2000,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2024 THEN population END) AS pop_2024
    FROM countries
    JOIN peers USING (country)
    GROUP BY country
)
SELECT
    country,
    round(kwh_2000, 0)                                         AS kwh_per_person_2000,
    round(kwh_2024, 0)                                         AS kwh_per_person_2024,
    round(kwh_2024 / kwh_2000, 2)                              AS times_bigger,
    -- average yearly growth over 24 years
    round(100 * (power(kwh_2024 / kwh_2000, 1e0 / 24) - 1), 1) AS growth_pct_per_year,
    round(pop_2000 / 1e6, 0)                                   AS population_2000_m,
    round(pop_2024 / 1e6, 0)                                   AS population_2024_m
FROM ends
ORDER BY kwh_per_person_2024;

Grid electricity per person since 2000. Nigeria (blue) and Bangladesh (orange) started at the same level.

The question

In 2000, the average person in Nigeria and the average person in Bangladesh got about the same amount of electricity from the national grid. Today, someone in Bangladesh gets more than three times as much. I wanted to know why.

What I found

It isn't because Nigeria is poorer. In 2022, people in both countries earned about the same on average, and most countries at that income level get far more power than Nigeria does. Nigeria now makes 2.5 times more electricity than it did in 2000, but there are also 1.8 times more people, so each person only gets a little more than before.

What it means

Nigeria's power supply has to grow much faster than its population. Solar is an obvious gap. It makes up less than 1% of the grid today.

03_commodity_change.sql
-- 03_commodity_change.sql
-- For each food: price in Jan 2023, its peak since then, and where it is now (Jul 2026).
WITH since_2023 AS (
    SELECT * FROM national_long WHERE month >= DATE '2023-01-01'
),
peaks AS (
    -- the month each food hit its highest price (the earliest one, if it hit it twice)
    SELECT food, month AS peak_month
    FROM (
        SELECT food, month, row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY food ORDER BY price DESC, month) AS rn
        FROM since_2023
    ) AS ranked
    WHERE rn = 1
),
per_food AS (
    SELECT
        s.food,
        max(CASE WHEN s.month = DATE '2023-01-01' THEN s.price END)  AS jan_2023,
        max(s.price)                                                 AS peak,
        max(p.peak_month)                                            AS peak_month,
        max(CASE WHEN s.month = DATE '2026-07-01' THEN s.price END)  AS jul_2026
    FROM since_2023 s
    JOIN peaks p ON p.food = s.food
    GROUP BY s.food
)
SELECT
    f.label                                                  AS food,
    p.food                                                   AS `column`,
    f.unit,
    CAST(round(jan_2023) AS SIGNED)                          AS price_jan_2023,
    CAST(round(peak) AS SIGNED)                              AS peak_price,
    DATE_FORMAT(peak_month, '%Y-%m')                         AS peak_month,
    CAST(round(jul_2026) AS SIGNED)                          AS price_jul_2026,
    CAST(round(100 * (peak / jan_2023 - 1)) AS SIGNED)       AS rise_to_peak_pct,
    CAST(round(100 * (jul_2026 / peak - 1)) AS SIGNED)       AS peak_to_now_pct,
    CAST(round(100 * (jul_2026 / jan_2023 - 1)) AS SIGNED)   AS jan_2023_to_now_pct
FROM per_food p
JOIN foods f ON f.food = p.food
ORDER BY jul_2026 / peak, f.label;

Staples like rice and beans came back down. Meat, eggs and milk stayed expensive.

The question

Food in Nigeria got very expensive in 2023 and 2024. I wanted to see how bad it really got, and whether prices are actually coming down now.

What I found

At the worst point, in mid-2024, food cost more than twice what it did a year before. Prices have been falling since August 2025, but food still costs about 2.5 times what it did in early 2023. Rice, beans and garri have come down a lot. Meat, eggs and milk have barely moved.

What it means

If you mostly buy staples, things are getting a bit easier. If you buy protein, they aren't. Where you live matters too: the same plate of rice, beans and garri costs about 25% more in the South South than in the North West.

02_calibration.sql
-- 02_calibration.sql
-- When the price said X%, how often did YES actually happen? With a 95% range (Wilson interval).
WITH b AS (
    SELECT horizon_days, bucket,
           count(*)             AS markets,
           avg(price_yes)       AS avg_price,
           avg(yes_won * 1e0)   AS yes_rate      -- * 1e0 keeps full precision (avg of whole numbers is cut to 4 places)
    FROM forecasts
    GROUP BY horizon_days, bucket
)
SELECT
    horizon_days,
    CONCAT(bucket * 10, ' to ', bucket * 10 + 10, '%') AS price_range,
    markets,
    round(100 * avg_price, 1) AS avg_price_pct,
    round(100 * yes_rate, 1)  AS happened_pct,
    round(100 * (yes_rate + POWER(1.96, 2) / (2 * markets)
        - 1.96 * sqrt(yes_rate * (1 - yes_rate) / markets + POWER(1.96, 2) / (4 * POWER(markets, 2))))
        / (1 + POWER(1.96, 2) / markets), 1) AS low_pct,
    round(100 * (yes_rate + POWER(1.96, 2) / (2 * markets)
        + 1.96 * sqrt(yes_rate * (1 - yes_rate) / markets + POWER(1.96, 2) / (4 * POWER(markets, 2))))
        / (1 + POWER(1.96, 2) / markets), 1) AS high_pct
FROM b
ORDER BY horizon_days DESC, bucket;

Each dot compares what the crowd predicted with what actually happened. Dots above the dashed line happened more often than predicted.

The question

On Polymarket, people bet real money on things like elections and football matches. If a bet costs 70 cents, the crowd is saying there's a 70% chance it happens. I checked whether those odds are actually right.

What I found

Mostly, yes, and they get more accurate as the event gets closer. But the crowd underrates underdogs. Things it gave about a 1 in 4 chance actually happened about 1 in 3 times.

What it means

The odds are a good guide, but don't write off the underdog. Politics was the hardest topic to call. On close races, the crowd did about as well as flipping a coin.

How late deliveries affect reviews and repeat orders

  • Olist

06_repeat_purchase.sql
-- 06_repeat_purchase.sql
-- Does a late FIRST delivery make a customer less likely to order again?
-- Only customers whose first order was before 2018-03-01 are included, so everyone
-- has at least 6 months in which a second order could appear.
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id, order_id, purchased_at, is_late, review_score,
           row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_unique_id ORDER BY purchased_at, order_id) AS n
    FROM fct_orders
),
firsts AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id, purchased_at AS first_at, is_late AS first_late,
           review_score AS first_review
    FROM ranked WHERE n = 1
),
repeaters AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT customer_unique_id FROM ranked WHERE n >= 2
)
SELECT
    CASE WHEN first_late THEN 'First order late' ELSE 'First order on time' END AS first_experience,
    count(*)                                                          AS customers,
    sum(CASE WHEN r.customer_unique_id IS NOT NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS repeat_customers,
    round(100.0 * avg(CASE WHEN r.customer_unique_id IS NOT NULL THEN 1e0 ELSE 0 END), 2) AS pct_repeat
FROM firsts f
LEFT JOIN repeaters r USING (customer_unique_id)
WHERE first_at < TIMESTAMP '2018-03-01 00:00:00'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

Average star rating, by how late the order arrived.

The question

Late deliveries annoy everyone. Using 96,203 orders from Olist, a Brazilian online marketplace, I measured what lateness actually costs a business.

What I found

Orders that arrived on time got an average of 4.3 stars out of 5. Late ones got 2.3. Even being a day or two late made bad reviews much more common. First-time buyers whose order came late were also about a quarter less likely to ever order again.

What it means

The fix is simpler than it sounds. Just 98 of the 2,945 sellers caused half of all late orders, so the store should keep a close eye on them, and give new customers delivery dates it can actually meet.
