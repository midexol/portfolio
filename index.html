03_then_and_now.sql
-- 03_then_and_now.sql
-- Where each peer started (2000), where it is now (2024), and how fast it grew.
WITH ends AS (
    SELECT
        country,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2000 THEN kwh_per_person END) AS kwh_2000,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2024 THEN kwh_per_person END) AS kwh_2024,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2000 THEN population END) AS pop_2000,
        max(CASE WHEN year = 2024 THEN population END) AS pop_2024
    FROM countries
    JOIN peers USING (country)
    GROUP BY country
)
SELECT
    country,
    round(kwh_2000, 0)                                         AS kwh_per_person_2000,
    round(kwh_2024, 0)                                         AS kwh_per_person_2024,
    round(kwh_2024 / kwh_2000, 2)                              AS times_bigger,
    -- average yearly growth over 24 years
    round(100 * (power(kwh_2024 / kwh_2000, 1e0 / 24) - 1), 1) AS growth_pct_per_year,
    round(pop_2000 / 1e6, 0)                                   AS population_2000_m,
    round(pop_2024 / 1e6, 0)                                   AS population_2024_m
FROM ends
ORDER BY kwh_per_person_2024;

Nigeria and Bangladesh started level in 2000. By 2024, Bangladesh had 3.6 times the grid power per person.

The question

In 2000, Nigeria and Bangladesh both made about 117 kWh of grid power per person. By 2024, Bangladesh made 586 and Nigeria made 161. Is that just because Nigeria is poorer?

What I found

No. In 2022 both had about the same income per person, around $5,080. Countries at that income make about 620 kWh on average. Bangladesh makes 604. Nigeria makes 170, the sixth lowest of 154 countries. Nigeria's output grew 2.5x since 2000, but its population grew 1.8x.

What it means

Income doesn't explain the gap. Power has to grow much faster than the population, and solar is only 0.3% of the grid.

03_commodity_change.sql
-- 03_commodity_change.sql
-- For each food: price in Jan 2023, its peak since then, and where it is now (Jul 2026).
WITH since_2023 AS (
    SELECT * FROM national_long WHERE month >= DATE '2023-01-01'
),
peaks AS (
    -- the month each food hit its highest price (the earliest one, if it hit it twice)
    SELECT food, month AS peak_month
    FROM (
        SELECT food, month, row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY food ORDER BY price DESC, month) AS rn
        FROM since_2023
    ) AS ranked
    WHERE rn = 1
),
per_food AS (
    SELECT
        s.food,
        max(CASE WHEN s.month = DATE '2023-01-01' THEN s.price END)  AS jan_2023,
        max(s.price)                                                 AS peak,
        max(p.peak_month)                                            AS peak_month,
        max(CASE WHEN s.month = DATE '2026-07-01' THEN s.price END)  AS jul_2026
    FROM since_2023 s
    JOIN peaks p ON p.food = s.food
    GROUP BY s.food
)
SELECT
    f.label                                                  AS food,
    p.food                                                   AS `column`,
    f.unit,
    CAST(round(jan_2023) AS SIGNED)                          AS price_jan_2023,
    CAST(round(peak) AS SIGNED)                              AS peak_price,
    DATE_FORMAT(peak_month, '%Y-%m')                         AS peak_month,
    CAST(round(jul_2026) AS SIGNED)                          AS price_jul_2026,
    CAST(round(100 * (peak / jan_2023 - 1)) AS SIGNED)       AS rise_to_peak_pct,
    CAST(round(100 * (jul_2026 / peak - 1)) AS SIGNED)       AS peak_to_now_pct,
    CAST(round(100 * (jul_2026 / jan_2023 - 1)) AS SIGNED)   AS jan_2023_to_now_pct
FROM per_food p
JOIN foods f ON f.food = p.food
ORDER BY jul_2026 / peak, f.label;

Staples came back down after their 2024 peak. Meat, eggs and milk stayed near the top.

The question

How much did food prices in Nigeria rise, and are they really coming down?

What I found

Food inflation peaked at 112% in June 2024. Prices have been falling since August 2025, but they're still about 2.5x early 2023. Rice, beans and garri are about 35% below their peak. Meat, eggs and milk are only 4% below.

What it means

Staples are getting cheaper. Protein isn't. The same plate of rice, beans and garri costs ₦4,011 in the South South and ₦3,208 in the North West.

02_calibration.sql
-- 02_calibration.sql
-- When the price said X%, how often did YES actually happen? With a 95% range (Wilson interval).
WITH b AS (
    SELECT horizon_days, bucket,
           count(*)             AS markets,
           avg(price_yes)       AS avg_price,
           avg(yes_won * 1e0)   AS yes_rate      -- * 1e0 keeps full precision (avg of whole numbers is cut to 4 places)
    FROM forecasts
    GROUP BY horizon_days, bucket
)
SELECT
    horizon_days,
    CONCAT(bucket * 10, ' to ', bucket * 10 + 10, '%') AS price_range,
    markets,
    round(100 * avg_price, 1) AS avg_price_pct,
    round(100 * yes_rate, 1)  AS happened_pct,
    round(100 * (yes_rate + POWER(1.96, 2) / (2 * markets)
        - 1.96 * sqrt(yes_rate * (1 - yes_rate) / markets + POWER(1.96, 2) / (4 * POWER(markets, 2))))
        / (1 + POWER(1.96, 2) / markets), 1) AS low_pct,
    round(100 * (yes_rate + POWER(1.96, 2) / (2 * markets)
        + 1.96 * sqrt(yes_rate * (1 - yes_rate) / markets + POWER(1.96, 2) / (4 * POWER(markets, 2))))
        / (1 + POWER(1.96, 2) / markets), 1) AS high_pct
FROM b
ORDER BY horizon_days DESC, bucket;

Each dot is a price range. On the dashed line means the price was exactly right. The underdogs sit well above it.

The question

When Polymarket prices something at 70%, does it happen about 70% of the time?

What I found

Mostly, yes. On the same 1,347 markets, the error score drops from 0.074 a month out to 0.038 the day before. Underdogs are the exception. Bets priced at 10 to 40% happened 35% of the time, not the 23% the price suggested.

What it means

The price is a good guide, except for underdogs. Politics was the weakest topic. On close calls it scored 0.249, about the same as guessing 50% every time.

06_repeat_purchase.sql
-- 06_repeat_purchase.sql
-- Does a late FIRST delivery make a customer less likely to order again?
-- Only customers whose first order was before 2018-03-01 are included, so everyone
-- has at least 6 months in which a second order could appear.
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id, order_id, purchased_at, is_late, review_score,
           row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY customer_unique_id ORDER BY purchased_at, order_id) AS n
    FROM fct_orders
),
firsts AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id, purchased_at AS first_at, is_late AS first_late,
           review_score AS first_review
    FROM ranked WHERE n = 1
),
repeaters AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT customer_unique_id FROM ranked WHERE n >= 2
)
SELECT
    CASE WHEN first_late THEN 'First order late' ELSE 'First order on time' END AS first_experience,
    count(*)                                                          AS customers,
    sum(CASE WHEN r.customer_unique_id IS NOT NULL THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS repeat_customers,
    round(100.0 * avg(CASE WHEN r.customer_unique_id IS NOT NULL THEN 1e0 ELSE 0 END), 2) AS pct_repeat
FROM firsts f
LEFT JOIN repeaters r USING (customer_unique_id)
WHERE first_at < TIMESTAMP '2018-03-01 00:00:00'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

Reviews collapse once a parcel is a few days late.

The question

How much does a late delivery hurt reviews and repeat orders? I used 96,203 orders from Olist, a Brazilian marketplace.

What I found

On-time orders average 4.28 stars. Late ones average 2.26. Even 1 to 3 days late triples the share of 1 and 2 star reviews. First-time buyers with a late order were 25% less likely to order again.

What it means

Just 98 of 2,945 sellers cause half of all late orders. Watch those sellers, and give first-time buyers delivery dates you can actually hit.
